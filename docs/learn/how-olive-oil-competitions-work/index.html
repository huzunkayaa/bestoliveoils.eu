How olive oil competitions work, and what a Gold is worth

A medal on a bottle is the one claim that did not come from the producer. It is still worth reading carefully: the competitions differ in how they judge, how many medals they hand out and whether they publish a table at all.

1. How judging works

Nearly every serious competition judges blind, in panels of trained tasters, using the IOC’s method or a close variant: the oil is warmed in a covered blue glass so colour cannot be seen, the taster scores fruitiness, bitterness and pungency and checks for defects, and the panel’s median decides. Most add a 100-point scale on top for ranking and medal thresholds. Entries are paid — typically a few hundred euros per oil — which means the field is self-selected: producers enter the oils they expect to win.

The differences are in what happens next. Some competitions give a medal to every oil over a threshold, so a vintage with many good entries produces many Golds. Some rank. Some score every entry and publish the score. One, WBOO, does not taste at all but totals other competitions’ results. Those four designs produce four different kinds of claim on a label.

Four designs, four meanings. A Gold from the first kind and a first place from the fourth are not the same achievement.

3. What a Gold means

At a threshold competition a Gold means the panel scored the oil above a fixed line — 85 or 90 out of 100, depending on the event — and found no defect. It is a real mark of quality. It is not a ranking: NYIOOC hands out several hundred Golds a year, London and Athena their share, and an oil with Golds from three competitions has passed three bars rather than beaten three fields. The scarce results are the ones on top of the medal — Best in Class, Best of Country, Best Koroneiki in the World — which single out one oil from the Golds, and the ranked competitions where there is a first and only one.

A Gold says the oil cleared the bar. “Best in Class” says it was the best oil that did.

Two more things to weigh. The year: a 2017 Best in Class on a 2025 bottle is history. And which oil: producers enter one bottling and print the medal on the range, so check the competition’s own list for the exact name — which is why we link to it wherever we can.

4. Reading a medal on a label

Find the competition, the year and the tier, in that order. A named competition from the list on our rankings hub is worth more than an unfamiliar one; the current or previous harvest year is worth more than an older one; a special award or a ranked place is worth more than a medal. Then look for what the medal cannot tell you — harvest date, variety, a laboratory figure — because a competition judged a sample once and you are buying a bottle now.

5. What we republish

The rankings hub lists the eighteen competitions and guides we follow, with each one’s organiser, scale, season and how many of the library’s oils cite it. Where we have republished a full results table — the WBOO 2025/26 ranking so far — it is reproduced in the competition’s own order with a link to our page on every oil we cover. We never reorder a table, and a medal is never our score: the panel rating on an oil page is ours, the medals are theirs, and the page keeps them apart.
