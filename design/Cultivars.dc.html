Kalamon

also called Kalamata, Kalamata olive, Aetonychi

Kalamon is grown above all as a table olive, the large almond-shaped black fruit sold across Europe as Kalamata. A small quantity of oil is pressed from it, usually in blends rather than as a monovarietal, which is why published varietal data on the oil is thin.

Commonly confused with Kalamata PDO olive oil, which is pressed from Koroneiki The protected designation refers to the region around Kalamata, not to the Kalamon variety. A bottle labelled Kalamata PDO is almost always Koroneiki oil. See Koroneiki →

In the grove

Kalamon grows across Messenia, Laconia and Aetolia, on hillsides where the summer is long enough to ripen the fruit fully but the autumn stays dry. The tree is vigorous and the fruit unusually large for a Greek variety, which is precisely what the table trade wants and what makes mechanical harvesting difficult: most of the crop is still picked by hand into baskets to avoid bruising.

Because the fruit is worth more cured than pressed, oil from Kalamon is usually a by-product — fruit rejected for size or blemish, pressed alongside other varieties. Monovarietal Kalamon oil exists but is rare enough that the literature carries no agreed sensory or phenolic profile for it.
